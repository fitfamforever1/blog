## Disclaimer

**What this is:** An examination of how religion, when enforced through law, politics and social pressure, affects education, scientific progress, women's participation, minority rights and public health in Pakistan. The argument draws on government statistics, academic research and reports from organisations such as the World Bank, UNICEF, the World Economic Forum, Pew Research Center and human-rights organisations.

**What this isn't:** An attack on Islam, the Quran, Muhammad or anyone's personal faith. We distinguish faith, which is a privately held conviction, from the use of religion as an instrument of state power. Our criticism is directed at laws, institutions and political decisions, not at people for what they believe.

**What we don't claim:** That religion is the only cause of Pakistan's problems. Poverty, weak governance, military influence in politics, feudal land ownership, debt and inadequate public services are also major factors. The argument is that religious authority can reinforce existing problems and make necessary reforms harder to pursue.

---

One of the most revealing questions about a society is not how devout its people are, but what happens when doctrine and evidence collide. Who wins?

If arithmetic says 2 + 2 = 4 and authority insists on 5, a country can absorb the cost for a while. But the bill compounds. In Pakistan, religion is more than a private conviction. It has a constitutional, legal and political role, influencing education, legislation, minority rights and the boundaries of acceptable public debate.

This does not mean religion explains every failure in Pakistan, nor that religious belief is inherently incompatible with scientific progress. It means that when religious authority is used to restrict criticism, justify unequal treatment or obstruct evidence-based policy, its consequences deserve scrutiny.

The pattern becomes visible across education, science, gender equality, minority rights and public health. These problems have multiple causes, but religiously justified laws and political pressure can make some of them harder to address.

## 1. Education: A country cannot build its future on empty classrooms

A nation builds its future in its classrooms, and Pakistan is still failing millions of children.

According to the Pakistani government's [Economic Survey 2025–26, Education chapter](https://www.finance.gov.pk/survey/chapter_26/Complete_PES2025_26.pdf), the reported literacy rate is 63%, with a rate of 73% for men and 54% for women. The survey also reports that 28% of school-age children are out of school.

UNICEF's [State of Children in Pakistan 2024 report](https://www.unicef.org/pakistan/media/7456/file/State-of-Children-Report%20Final%20%282%29.pdf.pdf) estimated that 26.089 million children aged 5–16 were out of school, or 38% of that age group. These estimates come from different reporting periods and methodologies, so they should not be treated as directly interchangeable. Together, however, they demonstrate the scale of Pakistan's educational crisis.

Poverty, child labour, inadequate school infrastructure, teacher shortages, gender discrimination and weak public services all contribute to this failure. Religion alone cannot explain why a child lacks a classroom or why a family cannot afford education.

But priorities matter. When political debate gives enormous attention to ideological conformity while millions of children remain outside the education system, we should ask what the state considers important. Religious education is not inherently incompatible with science or critical thinking. The problem begins when ideological priorities displace evidence-based teaching, critical inquiry or the practical work of educating every child.

Pakistan's education crisis is not proof that religion caused every school to fail. It is a reason to question any political arrangement in which demonstrating ideological commitment can matter more than delivering measurable public benefits.

A country cannot compete in a knowledge-driven world if its children are denied the opportunity to acquire knowledge in the first place.

## 2. Science: What happens when identity matters more than achievement?

A society's relationship with knowledge can be seen in how it treats its best minds.

Abdus Salam shared the 1979 Nobel Prize in Physics for his work on electroweak unification. He remains Pakistan's only Nobel laureate in the sciences. Yet his Ahmadi identity became a source of controversy in his relationship with Pakistan.

The [Second Amendment to Pakistan's Constitution, 1974](https://www.pakistani.org/pakistan/constitution/amendments/2amendment.html) classified Ahmadis as non-Muslims under Pakistani law. After Salam's death, the word "Muslim" was removed from the inscription on his gravestone, which had described him as the first Muslim Nobel laureate. His scientific achievement did not protect him from the consequences of a political and religious classification.

For further context, see the [Nobel Foundation's biography of Abdus Salam](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1979/salam/facts/).

The issue is not whether a scientist must hold any particular religious belief. It is that a person's religious identity can become a political test of belonging, even when that person has made an internationally recognised contribution to human knowledge.

The same danger appears in the 2017 killing of university student Mashal Khan. He was murdered by a mob following blasphemy allegations. An official investigation found no evidence supporting the accusations against him. [Al Jazeera's reporting on the investigation](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2017/6/5/lynched-pakistani-man-did-not-commit-blasphemy) documents the case.

These are different cases, but both raise a troubling question: what happens when religious identity or an accusation becomes more powerful than a person's achievements, rights or the evidence against them?

Scientific progress depends on asking questions, challenging established claims and correcting mistakes. When questioning authority carries serious social or legal risks, the damage extends beyond the individual. It threatens the intellectual freedom on which research and innovation depend.

Pakistan's scientific challenges also involve inadequate research funding, limited institutional capacity, brain drain and weak investment in higher education. These factors should not be reduced to religion. But a society that makes intellectual freedom conditional on religious conformity creates an additional obstacle to scientific progress.

## 3. Blasphemy laws: When an accusation becomes a weapon

Pakistan's blasphemy laws show how religious authority can acquire legal force, with consequences extending far beyond the courtroom.

Several of the harsher provisions were introduced or strengthened under General Zia-ul-Haq's military government. Section 295-C, concerning derogatory remarks about the Prophet Muhammad, was added to the Pakistan Penal Code in 1986 and provides for the death penalty. The historical development of these laws is documented in [Human Rights Watch's report on Pakistan's blasphemy laws](https://www.hrw.org/reports/1993/pakistan/).

The problem is not merely the severity of the legal penalties. Accusations can expose people to imprisonment, intimidation and mob violence before a court has established their guilt.

According to figures published by the Lahore-based Centre for Social Justice and reported by [Dawn in April 2025](https://www.dawn.com/news/1904988), at least 2,793 people had been charged under blasphemy laws since 1987. The organisation recorded 104 extrajudicial killings linked to blasphemy allegations between 1994 and 2024. It also documented 344 new cases in 2024.

These figures concern accusations and recorded killings, not convictions. They do not establish that every accusation was fabricated, but they demonstrate why due process and protection from mob violence are essential.

The 2024 figures also challenge the assumption that this is simply a conflict between Muslims and religious minorities. Approximately 70% of those accused were Muslims, while Ahmadis accounted for 14%, Christians 6% and Hindus 9%, according to the reported breakdown. The percentages are rounded. The fact that most recorded suspects were Muslim indicates that these laws also operate within the Muslim majority.

Religious minorities nevertheless face particular vulnerabilities. Christians make up a small share of Pakistan's population, while Ahmadis face constitutionally mandated religious exclusion and additional legal restrictions.

The consequences become especially alarming when allegations trigger violence before any judicial determination. In 2017, Mashal Khan was murdered after a false accusation. In June 2024, a tourist in Swat was killed by a mob following an allegation of Quran desecration. In August 2023, a mob attacked Christian communities in Jaranwala, damaging churches and homes after blasphemy allegations against two Christian men.

Sources: [Al Jazeera on the Mashal Khan investigation](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2017/6/5/lynched-pakistani-man-did-not-commit-blasphemy), [Dawn on the Swat lynching](https://www.dawn.com/news/1839865) and [Human Rights Watch on the Jaranwala attacks](https://www.hrw.org/world-report/2024/country-chapters/pakistan).

The central issue is not whether religious beliefs deserve respect. It is whether an accusation should be allowed to replace evidence, due process and the rule of law.

When the perceived defence of a sacred idea becomes a justification for denying another person basic rights, the law stops functioning as an adequate safeguard against injustice. It can instead become a source of fear.

## 4. Gender equality: Half the population is not spare capacity

No country can leave a substantial share of its talent underused and expect to compete with the world.

The [World Economic Forum's Global Gender Gap Report 2026](https://www.weforum.org/publications/global-gender-gap-report-2026/in-full/benchmarking-gender-gaps-2026/) ranked Pakistan 143rd out of 148 economies, with a gender-parity score of 59.5%. Pakistan performed particularly poorly in economic participation and opportunity, ranking 144th globally.

This ranking does not establish that religion is the sole cause of gender inequality. Poverty, inadequate transport, safety concerns, limited childcare, unequal access to education and weak enforcement of existing protections also constrain women's opportunities. The question is how religious interpretations used in law and politics interact with those existing barriers.

Women in Pakistan face obstacles to education, employment, leadership and economic independence. Some restrictions are defended through religious arguments about gender roles, guardianship and family responsibilities. Others persist because of economic dependency, social expectations or institutional failures.

The distinction between private faith and political power matters here. A person is free to choose a religiously informed way of life. But should one interpretation of religion determine the opportunities and rights available to everyone else?

The 2025 child-marriage debate offers a concrete example. Pakistan's Parliament passed the [Islamabad Capital Territory Child Marriage Restraint Act, 2025](https://www.na.gov.pk/uploads/documents/68be5f3758399_127.pdf), setting 18 as the minimum marriage age in the federal capital. The Council of Islamic Ideology declared the legislation un-Islamic, objecting to the treatment of marriages involving people under 18 and the bill's provisions concerning rape and penalties.

The [UK Home Office's 2025 country information report on Pakistan](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/pakistan-country-policy-and-information-notes/country-policy-and-information-note-women-fearing-gender-based-violence-pakistan-november-2022-accessible) documents the legislation and the Council's position. The law applies to the Islamabad Capital Territory; it should not be misrepresented as automatically establishing the same legal regime throughout every province.

The policy question is straightforward: how should the state protect children from coercion, safeguard their health and education, and ensure that they can develop into adults with meaningful choices?

These questions require evidence about children's welfare, not merely a contest over which interpretation of religious doctrine should prevail.

UNICEF defines child marriage as a marriage or union before the age of 18 and documents its consequences for children's wellbeing, education and development in its [Pakistan child-marriage country profile](https://www.unicef.org/pakistan/documents/child-marriage-country-profile-pakistan).

Early marriage can interrupt education and limit future opportunities. When girls leave school early and women are excluded from the workforce, the country loses skills, income and potential contributions to public life. These losses compound an already serious development problem.

Religious belief does not automatically produce these outcomes. The problem is when religiously justified restrictions are allowed to override the rights and welfare of the people affected by them.

## 5. Public health: A vaccine cannot protect a child it never reaches

Public health tests whether a society can turn evidence into action.

Pakistan and Afghanistan remain the only countries where wild poliovirus is endemic. Pakistan recorded 74 cases in 2024 and 31 in 2025, according to the [Pakistan Polio Eradication Programme](https://www.poliofreepakistan.gov.pk/).

The persistence of polio is not simply a story about religion. Insecurity, attacks on vaccination teams, missed vaccination campaigns, gaps in routine immunisation, population movement and operational difficulties all contribute to continued transmission.

However, religious misinformation and militant propaganda have also undermined vaccination efforts. Some campaigns have been portrayed as foreign plots or as threats to religious identity, making it harder for health workers to reach every child. These claims should be evaluated against medical evidence, not accepted simply because they invoke religious authority.

The [World Health Organization's polio information](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/poliomyelitis) explains that vaccination is central to preventing the disease and that multiple doses are needed to provide children with adequate protection.

The point is not that religion is inherently opposed to medicine. Religious leaders and communities can support vaccination, and many do. The problem arises when ideological claims discourage effective healthcare, put frontline workers at risk or prevent children from receiving protection.

Public-health decisions should be guided by medical evidence, transparent communication and the protection of patients and health workers. A child's health should not become collateral damage in a political or ideological struggle.

## 6. Public opinion: Why reform becomes politically difficult

None of these issues exists in a political vacuum. Politicians respond to voters, organised groups, religious authorities and the social costs of challenging established beliefs.

A [2013 Pew Research Center survey](https://www.pewresearch.org/religion/2013/04/30/the-worlds-muslims-religion-politics-society/) found that 84% of Pakistani Muslims surveyed favoured making sharia the official law of the land. Among Pakistani Muslims who supported making sharia official law, 76% supported the death penalty for people who left Islam for another faith.

These figures are more than a decade old. They should not be presented as a measurement of Pakistani public opinion in 2026, and the survey does not establish that every respondent understood sharia in the same way. It also does not show that every supporter of sharia opposes science, education or human rights.

Nevertheless, the findings raise an important political question. When religious principles are widely understood as a basis for legislation, how much room do elected representatives have to reform laws that influential groups regard as religious obligations?

The difficulty arises when a particular interpretation is treated as beyond criticism and disagreement is framed as an attack on religion itself. Politicians may then face pressure not only to defend a policy's practical consequences but also to prove that it conforms to a particular religious position.

This can distort public debate. Instead of asking whether a law protects children, reduces violence or improves public health, the argument becomes a contest over whether the reform is sufficiently religious.

That is a problem of accountability. Laws affect believers and non-believers alike, so their consequences must remain open to public scrutiny.

The solution is not to assume that religious people are opposed to reform. It is to ensure that no religious or political authority is exempt from questions about the effects of the policies it advocates.

## Conclusion: Evidence must have the final word

Pakistan's problems cannot be solved by blaming religion for everything. Poverty, weak governance, debt, inequality, political interference and institutional failure are real and substantial causes of the country's difficulties.

But acknowledging those causes does not require us to ignore the damage that religiously justified laws and political pressure can inflict. The constitutional exclusion of Ahmadis, the dangers created by blasphemy accusations, resistance to raising the minimum marriage age and opposition to evidence-based public-health measures all illustrate the stakes.

The evidence does not prove that religion is the sole cause of Pakistan's educational, scientific or economic difficulties. It does show that religious authority has influenced specific laws and policy debates, sometimes in ways that conflict with equality, due process and the welfare of vulnerable people.

Private belief is a personal matter. State power is not.

When the state makes decisions that determine people's education, health, rights and opportunities, those decisions should be justified by evidence, open to criticism and subject to public scrutiny. No interpretation of doctrine should automatically exempt a law from questions about its consequences.

Because when evidence says 4 and authority insists on 5, the important question is not which belief feels more sacred.

It is what the country pays for the difference.
